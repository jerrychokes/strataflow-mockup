## Part 1 — the four things round 1 left

### 1. W23-1's fix, under its own mutation — correct where it was scoped, and the scope was too narrow

Moved `INDETERMINATE[0]`'s criterion (`ANALYTES`'s `Cadmium (filtered)` entry,
`a: '0.54'`) to **0.3** and separately to **2.0** on two scratch copies, rebuilt
both, and read the four-rule table on `#map` and the badges beside it.

**At 0.3** (limit 1.0 stays above the criterion — the "same side" case): all
four rows read correctly. Rule 1 stays *indeterminate*; rules 2 and 3 both read
*exceedance* and the half-limit row correctly says *"the same answer as the
rule above, which is what happens when the criterion sits outside the range
the two substitutions span"* — true, 0.3 is outside [0.5, 1.0].

**At 2.0** (limit 1.0 now falls *below* the criterion — the case round 1 named
and nobody had tested): this is where it gets interesting.

| Rule | Comparison word | Verdict word | Badge tone/glyph | "Same answer" clause |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Left as reported | "the limit 1 is below the criterion 2" — correct | flips `indeterminate` → `compliant`, correctly, and says why (*"conclusively compliant… the hatched mark would be overstating the doubt"*) | flips `good`(●) → `warn`(◐) via a getter — correct | n/a |
| Substituted at limit | "1 is below 2" — correct | flips `exceedance` → `compliant` — correct | **stays `bad`(■) — hardcoded, not derived** | n/a |
| Substituted at half-limit | "0.5 is below 2" — correct | flips `compliant` → `compliant` (unchanged) | **stays `bad`(■) — hardcoded** | "the same answer as the rule above… outside the range" — correct, 2.0 > 1.0 |
| Excluded | unchanged | `absent`, unchanged | `bad`(■), unchanged, disclosed as structural | n/a |

The **words** — comparison, verdict, and the "same answer" clause — are
correct in all four rules, under both directions of mutation. That part of
W23-1 is solid and I could not break it.

**The tone is not fully derived, and the response's own claim overstates what
was done.** The response says *"Only excluded keeps a literal, because
producing no mark is structural rather than arithmetic, and it says so."*
That is not accurate: rules 2 and 3 also keep a literal — their `tone: 'bad'`
field, unlike `reads`/`says`, was never converted to a getter. Under the
mutation this produces a badge that says **"compliant"** in the app's `bad`
styling (`color: var(--mk-bad); font-weight: 600`, glyph `■`) — the same
visual language the rest of the catalogue reserves for `exceedance`/`fail`
(`toneFor('compliant')` returns `'good'` everywhere else in the codebase;
`status()`'s own docstring is *"a shape in front of it, so greyscale keeps the
meaning"*, and here the shape and the word disagree).

**This is not hypothetical — it is live on the shipped page today.** At the
shipped criterion (0.54), the half-limit row (0.5) already reads `compliant`
with a `bad`/■ badge (screenshot: `map-375-nondetect.png`, `map-1280-nondetect.png`).
A practitioner scanning this table — which the wave itself calls the single
most valuable thing on the screen — by colour first would read three red
squares in a row (`exceedance`, `compliant`, `absent`) and reasonably wonder
why the middle one says "compliant."

I can read this two ways. Read one: it is a bug — the fix should have derived
`tone` the same way it derived `reads`. Read two: it is a deliberate,
defensible choice — `tone` in rows 2–4 encodes *"this substitution methodology
is not to be trusted,"* not *"this is the compliance state,"* and derives its
tone from *which rule produced it* rather than *what the rule says*, which is
consistent with the whole point of the table (four arbitrary rules, three of
them worth doubting regardless of what they answer). I lean toward read two
being defensible as shipped, but the response's specific sentence — *"only
excluded keeps a literal"* — is not true of the code, and given how much
weight this audit chain puts on exactly that kind of overclaim (W23-1 itself
was one), I am filing it. **W23-R2-2, P2**, not P1: no word or number goes
false, only a colour convention gets reused for a second meaning without
saying so.
